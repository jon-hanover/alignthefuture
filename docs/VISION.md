## The need

Perhaps the biggest challenge facing the next generation is whether we manage
to align AI with human interests. Whether it serves humanity, or humanity ends
up serving it. And we can't just say that's going to be handled by some
technocrat in Washington or an AI researcher in a lab.

That's a democratic process. It's going to take all of us. Lots of people, at
lots of different levels, in lots of different positions throughout the economy
and throughout society, who understand the challenges of AI alignment and have
the skills, mindsets and knowledge to set the right policies, demand the right
research, and understand that research well enough to act on it. That's how we
create the future we actually want. That's the problem area we're addressing.

One other thing. My suspicion is that this is also where a lot of the jobs
created by AI are going to be. Alignment is the one thing we will not want to
delegate to AI, because it is the work of deciding what AI should be doing in
the first place. What guardrails do we set? How do we know whether a system is
advancing our goals or undermining them? [? the original audio here was
"medium or needs for liberating our goals," which I read as serving versus
subverting]

It strikes me as essential that we keep humans at that wheel, and my gut is we
will. So this is also about getting kids ready for the actual AI economy. That
economy will be less about using AI to accomplish knowledge work tasks, because
AI will be doing that itself, and more about the governance, oversight and
distinctly human activities that keep AI in line with human interests. That
work might happen in government. It might happen in companies. It might happen
in the public square. It could end up being ubiquitous. [?]

## The program idea

Still fuzzy, but I'm imagining something like Model UN meets a robotics
competition. [? "robotic sponsors" in the transcript] Kids learn about how AI
research and safety research is actually conducted today: how the work
happens, where it works well, where it's gapped. But they also learn by doing.

Concretely, set up sandboxes where kids run simulated training sessions on
models, institute different policies, and see the trade-offs that come with
each one. Does this policy actually produce the outcome you wanted? Play those
scenarios out in a simulated global economy and society. Then layer on mock
debates around what they found.

The culminating project might be: you are now the committee in Congress that
has to write the AI alignment regulations. Debate it. Codify it. Gosh, does
that seem fun? And valuable and impactful.

## Open questions

These are the questions I want to sit with before designing anything further.
A lot of the answers are "I don't know yet," and that's fine. They're here so
the gaps are visible.

**Thesis.** The memo gives two reasons this matters: the civic one (alignment
is a democratic project and needs a broad base of literate citizens) and the
economic one (alignment is where the durable human jobs will be). Which one is
load-bearing? If a funder only bought one, which one would I want it to be?

**Audience.** Who exactly is "kids" here? Middle school, high school, early
college? Is this for the kids already drawn to robotics and Model UN, or is
the whole point to reach the ones who would never sign up for either?

**Outcome.** What does a kid walk away with? A credential, a portfolio
artifact, a set of skills, a changed sense of what they could do for a living?
And what would I measure to know it worked?

**Format.** Summer camp, after school, in-school elective, or all three over
time? The memo says the funding decides this. Is that true, or is there a
version I actually want and should lead with?

**Simulator.** The sandbox is the heart of the idea and the hardest part to
build. What is the minimum version of it? Could the first pilot run on paper,
tabletop-game style, with a real model only in the background? Or does the
"learn by doing" promise fall apart without real training runs?

**Curriculum.** How much of this is teaching kids how alignment research is
actually done today (interpretability, evals, RLHF, red teaming) versus
teaching them the policy and governance layer on top? Who on the technical
side would need to vet the content so it isn't cartoon alignment?

**Playlab.** Is Playlab the tool kids use, the tool teachers use to build the
simulator, or both? The teacher-builds-the-simulator idea is fun. Is it a
distraction from the first pilot?

**Partners.** Which partners are for credibility, which are for distribution,
and which are for money? Run The Future, Rithm, History CoLab, DMNS, OpenAI
and Anthropic all show up in the memo, but they play different roles. What is
the one partnership that would make everything else easier?

**Pilot.** What is the smallest real thing I could run in Denver next summer?
How many kids, how many days, what would it cost, and who hosts?

**Role.** Am I the founder of this, the incubator who hands it off, or the
advisor who helps someone else build it? Does it live inside a client
organization, a partner organization, or on its own?

**Name.** "Align the Future" is the domain. Is it the name of the program, the
movement, or just the placeholder? What does the landing page need to say to
the first three audiences it will meet (funders, partner organizations,
parents or teachers)?

**Timing.** Why now, and what happens if this waits a year? Is there a funding
cycle, a partner window, or a moment in the public conversation that makes
this year different?
